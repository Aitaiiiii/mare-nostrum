# Acute Limb Ischemia (ALI)

A sudden fall in limb perfusion threatening tissue viability — a **time-critical emergency measured in hours.** The reflex is to **anticoagulate immediately, classify the limb (Rutherford), and get vascular surgery/IR involved now**: a viable window missed becomes an amputation.

## 🧬 Aetiology & Pathophysiology

Abrupt arterial occlusion → distal ischaemia; tissue tolerance is short (skeletal muscle/nerve infarct by ~6 h). Two mechanisms:

| | **Embolic** | **Thrombotic (in situ)** |
|---|---|---|
| Source/setting | **cardiac — AF, post-MI mural thrombus**, valve, aneurysm; sudden | pre-existing atherosclerosis/PAD; graft/stent thrombosis; on a background of claudication |
| Onset | dramatic, no collaterals | often less severe (collaterals) |
| Contralateral limb | normal pulses | signs of chronic PAD |

Other causes: aortic dissection extending to a limb, trauma, popliteal aneurysm thrombosis, hypercoagulable states, and **phlegmasia cerulea dolens** (massive venous thrombosis). **Reperfusion** after revascularisation releases K⁺, myoglobin, and acid → hyperkalaemia, AKI (rhabdomyolysis), and **compartment syndrome**.

## 🩺 Clinical presentation — the 6 Ps

**Pain** (sudden, severe), **Pallor**, **Pulselessness**, **Perishingly cold (Poikilothermia)**, **Paraesthesia**, **Paralysis.** The **later Ps — sensory loss and motor weakness — signal an advanced, threatened limb** and drive urgency. Compare with the contralateral limb; a normal contralateral pulse exam suggests embolism, chronic PAD signs suggest in-situ thrombosis.

## Rutherford classification (drives the decision)

| Category | Limb | Sensory loss | Motor deficit | Doppler (arterial/venous) | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **I** viable | not threatened | none | none | both audible | not immediately threatened; image, treat within 24 h |
| **IIa** marginally threatened | salvageable if prompt | minimal (toes) | none | arterial often inaudible / venous audible | **revascularise < 6 h** |
| **IIb** immediately threatened | salvageable if immediate | more than toes, rest pain | mild–moderate | arterial inaudible / venous audible | **emergency revascularisation** |
| **III** irreversible | major tissue loss/permanent damage | profound, anaesthetic | profound, paralysis/**rigor** | both inaudible; **fixed mottling** | **primary amputation** (revascularisation harmful) |

## 🩻 Investigations (do not delay heparin)

Bedside **hand-held Doppler** (arterial + venous signals) is the key test to categorise. **CT angiography** defines the level/anatomy and planning (or catheter angiography, often at the time of intervention). Bloods: CBC, coagulation, **U&E, creatinine, K⁺, CK, lactate**, group & save; ECG (AF, MI); echocardiogram later for embolic source. In category III, imaging must not delay amputation.

## 💊 Management

**1. Immediate systemic anticoagulation.** **IV unfractionated heparin bolus 70–100 U/kg then infusion**, as soon as ALI is diagnosed — it does not lyse existing clot but limits propagation and preserves collateral/microcirculatory flow while revascularisation is arranged. Analgesia, IV fluids, oxygen, and keep the limb **dependent and warm (do not actively warm/cool aggressively).**

**2. Urgent vascular surgery / interventional radiology referral — revascularise by category & timing.**
- **IIa: within ~6 h; IIb: emergency (immediate).** Options: **surgical embolectomy (Fogarty catheter)** — classic for embolic occlusion; **bypass**; **endovascular** — catheter-directed thrombolysis, mechanical/aspiration thrombectomy, angioplasty/stent; or hybrid.
- **Category I:** treat within 24 h (image, anticoagulate, plan).
- **Category III (fixed mottling, anaesthesia, paralysis, muscle rigor):** revascularisation is futile/harmful → **primary amputation** + supportive care.

**3. Manage reperfusion.** Anticipate **compartment syndrome** (tense, pain on passive stretch, worsening after flow restored) → **fasciotomy**; treat **hyperkalaemia, myoglobinuric AKI (rhabdomyolysis)**, and metabolic acidosis; monitor CK/K⁺/renal function.

**4. Treat the cause & secondary prevention.** Anticoagulation for a cardiac embolic source (**AF → long-term** — see [[Atrial Fibrillation]], [[Anticoagulant Therapy]]); antiplatelet/statin and PAD management for atherothrombotic ALI ([[PAD (Peripheral Artery Disease)]]); investigate for aneurysm/hypercoagulability.

## 🇹🇭 Thailand availability

Heparin, thrombolytics, and both open (embolectomy/bypass) and endovascular revascularisation are available at Thai regional/tertiary vascular centres; peripheral hospitals should **heparinise and refer immediately.** AF-related embolism and atherosclerotic thrombosis are the dominant Thai causes; access to urgent vascular surgery can be the rate-limiting step, so early recognition + heparin + transfer is the key deliverable.

> [!danger] 🚨 Red flags / strict avoidance
> - **Heparinise the moment ALI is diagnosed** — do not wait for imaging or the vascular team.
> - **The clock is ~6 h for a threatened limb (IIa/IIb)** — sensory/motor loss means act now.
> - **Category III (fixed mottling, paralysis, rigor) → amputation** — revascularising a dead limb causes lethal reperfusion injury.
> - **Anticipate reperfusion**: hyperkalaemia, rhabdomyolysis/AKI, and **compartment syndrome → fasciotomy**.
> - Don't mistake it for DVT — **phlegmasia** is venous but ALI is arterial; both threaten the limb.

> [!tip] 🎯 High-Yield Recall
> - **6 Ps**; later Ps (paraesthesia/paralysis) = threatened limb.
> - **IV heparin 70–100 U/kg immediately**, then classify by **Rutherford + hand-held Doppler**.
> - **IIa/IIb → revascularise < 6 h** (embolectomy/thrombolysis/thrombectomy); **III → primary amputation**.
> - **Embolic (AF/cardiac, sudden, normal other leg)** vs **thrombotic (chronic PAD background)**.
> - Reperfusion → **hyperkalaemia, rhabdo/AKI, compartment syndrome (fasciotomy)**; anticoagulate the source (AF).
