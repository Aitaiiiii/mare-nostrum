# Acute Gastroenteritis & Infectious Diarrhea

Acute (< 14 days) diarrhoea from infection or toxin — one of the commonest ER/OPD presentations. The whole management pivots on two questions: **how dehydrated is the patient (→ rehydration)** and **is this watery or inflammatory diarrhoea (→ whether antibiotics are needed at all)**. The default answer on antibiotics is **no**.

## 🧬 Pathophysiology & the two syndromes

| | **Non-inflammatory (watery)** | **Inflammatory (dysenteric)** |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | enterotoxin / viral enterocyte damage, small bowel | mucosal invasion/cytotoxin, colon |
| Stool | large-volume watery, no blood | small-volume, **blood + mucus**, tenesmus |
| Fever | usually none/low | often high |
| Organisms | norovirus/rotavirus, ETEC, *V. cholerae*, *S. aureus*/*B. cereus* toxin, *Cryptosporidium* | *Shigella*, *Salmonella*, *Campylobacter*, EHEC/STEC, *Entamoeba histolytica*, *C. difficile* |
| Faecal leukocytes | absent | present |

The clinical danger is **dehydration** (hypovolaemia → AKI, shock) and, in specific settings, complications (HUS with STEC, bacteraemia in the immunocompromised, toxic megacolon).

## 🩺 Assessment — grade the dehydration

The single most important assessment is **volume status**:
- **None/mild:** normal vitals, moist mucosa, normal skin turgor, normal urine output.
- **Moderate:** tachycardia, dry mucosa, reduced turgor, oliguria, postural dizziness.
- **Severe:** hypotension, weak/absent pulses, sunken eyes, lethargy/altered mental status, anuria — **shock.**

Take a **food/travel/water/animal/antibiotic-exposure history**; note blood/mucus (dysentery), duration, and host factors (age extremes, immunocompromise, pregnancy, comorbidity).

## 🩻 Investigations (most cases need none)

- **Mild self-limited watery diarrhoea → no tests.**
- **Test when:** severe/dehydrated, **bloody diarrhoea (dysentery)**, fever, > 7 days, immunocompromised, recent hospital/antibiotics (*C. difficile*), or outbreak/public-health concern.
- **Stool exam/culture, faecal leukocytes/lactoferrin, ova & parasites** (*Entamoeba* in Thailand), ***C. difficile* toxin/PCR** if recent antibiotics, blood cultures if septic; **electrolytes, BUN/creatinine, venous gas** if dehydrated/vomiting (hypokalaemia, metabolic acidosis, AKI).

## 💊 Management

**1. Rehydration is the core treatment.**
- **Mild–moderate → oral rehydration solution (ORS, low-osmolarity WHO formula)** — the mainstay, effective even in cholera; encourage frequent small amounts, replace ongoing losses.
- **Severe/shock or unable to tolerate oral → IV isotonic crystalloid** (rapid boluses to restore perfusion, then replace deficit + ongoing losses); correct **potassium** and acidosis.
- **Continue feeding** (do not fast); resume a normal diet as tolerated.

**2. Antibiotics — the exception, not the rule.** Most acute diarrhoea (viral, toxin-mediated, mild bacterial) is **self-limiting and needs NO antibiotics.**
- **Consider empiric antibiotics for:** **dysentery** (bloody diarrhoea + fever, likely *Shigella*/invasive), severe disease with systemic toxicity, the immunocompromised, or suspected **cholera** (large-volume rice-water stool). Empiric choice: **a fluoroquinolone or azithromycin** (azithromycin preferred where *Campylobacter*/FQ resistance is high — common in Asia).
- **Amoebic dysentery** (*E. histolytica*, common in Thailand) → **metronidazole/tinidazole + a luminal agent (diloxanide/paromomycin).**
- ***C. difficile*** → stop the culprit antibiotic; oral vancomycin or fidaxomicin.
- **AVOID antibiotics if EHEC/STEC (O157) is suspected** (bloody diarrhoea, no high fever, outbreak) — antibiotics **increase the risk of haemolytic-uraemic syndrome (HUS)**.

**3. Adjuncts.** **Zinc supplementation in children** (reduces duration/severity). **Antimotility agents (loperamide)** may relieve mild watery diarrhoea but are **contraindicated in dysentery/suspected invasive or *C. difficile* infection** (risk of toxic megacolon/prolonged illness). Antiemetics (ondansetron) aid oral rehydration, especially in children.

## 🇹🇭 Thailand availability & context

ORS, IV crystalloids, azithromycin/ciprofloxacin, metronidazole, and zinc are all on the Thai NLEM and universally available. Thai-relevant causes: **amoebic and bacillary dysentery**, ***Vibrio* species** (including cholera and *V. parahaemolyticus* from seafood), *Salmonella*/*Campylobacter*, and, in returning travellers/rural exposure, parasitic causes — so **stool O&P and *Entamoeba* testing matter** in the dysenteric Thai patient. Overlapping febrile illnesses (dengue, typhoid, melioidosis, leptospirosis) must be considered when systemic features dominate — see [[Tropical Infectious Diseases]].

> [!danger] 🚨 Red flags / strict avoidance
> - **Rehydrate first** — dehydration/shock and AKI are what harm; ORS for most, IV for severe.
> - **Most cases need NO antibiotics** — reserve for dysentery/severe/immunocompromised/cholera.
> - **Do NOT give antibiotics if EHEC/STEC suspected** (bloody diarrhoea, outbreak) — precipitates HUS.
> - **No loperamide in dysentery / *C. difficile* / suspected invasive infection** (toxic megacolon).
> - Bloody diarrhoea + fever = dysentery → stool workup; consider amoebiasis in Thailand.

> [!tip] 🎯 High-Yield Recall
> - Classify **watery (viral/toxin, self-limited)** vs **inflammatory/dysenteric (blood+mucus+fever, invasive)**.
> - **Rehydration is the treatment**: **ORS (low-osmolarity WHO)** mild–moderate; **IV crystalloid** severe; keep feeding; replace K⁺.
> - **Antibiotics only for dysentery/severe/immunocompromised/cholera** (azithromycin or FQ); **amoebic → metronidazole + luminal agent**.
> - **No antibiotics if STEC/O157** (HUS); **no loperamide in dysentery/*C. diff***.
> - **Zinc in children**; ondansetron aids oral rehydration.
