# Hepatic Encephalopathy (HE)

A **reversible** neuropsychiatric syndrome of liver failure/portosystemic shunting, ranging from subtle cognitive change to coma. In a decompensating cirrhotic it is usually **triggered by an identifiable precipitant**, so the highest-yield action is not lowering ammonia per se but **hunting down and correcting the precipitant** while giving lactulose.

## 🧬 Pathophysiology

Failure of the liver to clear gut-derived neurotoxins — chiefly **ammonia** (from colonic bacterial urease and glutamine metabolism) — which crosses the blood–brain barrier → astrocyte glutamine accumulation, swelling, oxidative stress, and altered neurotransmission (↑GABAergic tone). Systemic inflammation and hyponatraemia amplify it. Note **the blood ammonia level correlates poorly with grade and does not guide therapy** — HE is a clinical diagnosis.

## Precipitants (find and treat — this is the management)

- **Infection** — especially [[Spontaneous Bacterial Peritonitis (SBP)|SBP]], UTI, pneumonia (tap the ascites!).
- **GI bleeding** ([[UGIB (Upper Gastrointestinal Bleeding)|variceal]]) — a large protein/blood load in the gut.
- **Constipation.**
- **Electrolyte/metabolic** — **hypokalaemia** (increases renal ammoniagenesis), hyponatraemia, alkalosis, dehydration (over-diuresis), AKI.
- **Sedatives/opioids/benzodiazepines**, non-adherence to lactulose.
- **TIPS/portosystemic shunt**, dietary protein excess (rarely the primary driver).

## 🩺 Presentation — West Haven grades

| Grade | Features |
|---|---|
| Minimal/covert | normal exam, abnormal psychometric tests only |
| I | mild confusion, altered sleep, short attention; subtle asterixis |
| II | lethargy, disorientation to time, **obvious asterixis**, personality change |
| III | somnolence → stupor, marked confusion, arousable |
| IV | coma |

**Asterixis (flapping tremor)** and **fetor hepaticus** are classic. Exclude other causes of altered mentation (hypoglycaemia, intracranial event, drugs, sepsis, Wernicke's) — HE is partly a diagnosis of exclusion.

## 🩻 Investigations

Aimed at the **precipitant**: **diagnostic paracentesis (SBP), septic screen (cultures, CXR, urine), CBC, electrolytes (K⁺/Na⁺), renal/liver function, glucose (exclude hypoglycaemia), coagulation**, and assess for GI bleeding. Imaging (CT head) if focal signs, trauma, or not responding. Ammonia is not required to diagnose or monitor.

## 💊 Management

**1. Treat the precipitant — the cornerstone.** Antibiotics for infection/SBP, control **GI bleeding**, relieve **constipation**, **correct hypokalaemia/hyponatraemia/dehydration**, stop **sedatives**, and manage AKI. Most episodes resolve when the trigger is fixed.

**2. Lactulose — first-line, titrated to stool output.** **Lactulose 30–45 mL PO/NG every 1–2 h until bowel movement, then titrate to 2–3 soft stools per day** (acidifies the colon → traps ammonia as ammonium + cathartic effect). For grade III–IV or the patient who can't take oral: **lactulose retention enemas** (300 mL in 700 mL water). Avoid over-purging (dehydration/hypokalaemia → worsens HE).

**3. Rifaximin — add for recurrence/inadequate response.** **Rifaximin 550 mg PO bid** (a non-absorbed antibiotic reducing ammoniagenic gut flora) added to lactulose reduces recurrence and hospitalisation; it is standard for secondary prevention.

**4. Airway & supportive.** **Grade III–IV → protect the airway (intubate)** for coma/aspiration risk; ICU. **Do NOT protein-restrict** — maintain adequate protein/nutrition (old protein-restriction dogma worsens outcomes); provide calories.

**5. Refractory/recurrent HE.** Consider precipitant-free causes (large spontaneous shunt — embolisation; TIPS-related — may need revision), LOLA where available, and **transplant assessment** (recurrent HE marks advanced disease).

## 🇹🇭 Thailand availability

Lactulose and rifaximin are available in Thailand (rifaximin cost/access varies); ascitic-fluid analysis and standard supportive care are routine. Cirrhosis from **alcohol, HBV/HCV, and NAFLD** is common, so HE is a frequent Thai ward presentation — the "find the precipitant (tap the ascites, look for a bleed, check K⁺)" discipline is the highest-yield habit.

> [!danger] 🚨 Red flags / strict avoidance
> - **Find and treat the precipitant** (SBP — tap the ascites!, GI bleed, constipation, hypokalaemia, sedatives) — this drives recovery.
> - **The ammonia level does NOT guide therapy** — HE is clinical; don't chase the number.
> - **Do NOT protein-restrict** — maintain nutrition; protein restriction worsens outcomes.
> - **Grade III–IV → protect the airway/ICU**; avoid over-purging with lactulose (dehydration/hypokalaemia rebound).
> - Exclude other causes of altered mentation (hypoglycaemia, intracranial event, Wernicke's, sepsis).

> [!tip] 🎯 High-Yield Recall
> - HE = reversible neuropsychiatric syndrome; **asterixis**, West Haven grade; **ammonia doesn't guide therapy**.
> - **Precipitants: infection/SBP, GI bleed, constipation, hypokalaemia, sedatives, dehydration** — treat them.
> - **Lactulose titrated to 2–3 soft stools/day** (enema if grade III–IV); **add rifaximin 550 mg bid** for recurrence.
> - **Grade III–IV → intubate/ICU**; **don't protein-restrict**; avoid over-purging.
> - Tap the ascites in any decompensating cirrhotic (SBP is the classic hidden trigger).
