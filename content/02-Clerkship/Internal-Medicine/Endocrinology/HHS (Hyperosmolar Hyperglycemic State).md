# HHS (Hyperosmolar Hyperglycemic State)

A hyperglycaemic emergency of (usually type 2) diabetes characterised by **profound hyperglycaemia, hyperosmolality, and severe dehydration with little or no ketoacidosis**, and depressed consciousness. It develops over days, the fluid deficits are enormous, and **mortality is higher than in [[DKA (Diabetic Ketoacidosis)|DKA]]** (older, comorbid patients) — so fluid is the priority and insulin is secondary and cautious.

## 🧬 Pathophysiology — why HHS differs from DKA

There is **enough residual insulin to suppress lipolysis/ketogenesis** (hence minimal ketosis/acidosis) but not enough to control hyperglycaemia. Sustained glycosuria → osmotic diuresis → **massive water and electrolyte loss** (deficits often ~8–12 L) → rising serum osmolality → cellular dehydration and the **CNS depression/coma** that correlates with osmolality. A precipitant (often infection) tips a patient with limited access to water (elderly, dependent) over the edge.

## 🩺 Clinical presentation & diagnostic criteria

Insidious onset over days: polyuria, polydipsia, progressive weakness, and **altered mental status/coma** (correlates with osmolality; seizures/focal signs can occur). Marked dehydration (dry, hypotension, tachycardia). **Diagnostic criteria (typical):**
- **Plasma glucose > 600 mg/dL (33.3 mmol/L)**
- **Effective serum osmolality > 320 mOsm/kg** (= 2×Na + glucose[mmol/L]; or 2×Na + glucose/18 in mg/dL)
- **Arterial pH > 7.30 and serum bicarbonate > 18 mmol/L** (minimal acidosis)
- **Minimal ketonaemia/ketonuria**
- Depressed consciousness common

Mixed HHS/DKA pictures occur. **Correct the sodium for hyperglycaemia** when tracking it.

## 🩻 Investigations

Glucose, **electrolytes (Na⁺ corrected, K⁺ — total-body depleted despite a "normal/high" measured value), urea/creatinine (often high — AKI), calculated osmolality, venous gas, ketones (blood β-hydroxybutyrate)**, CBC, and a **precipitant search: septic screen (culture, CXR, urine), ECG/troponin (silent MI), and CNS assessment.** Phosphate and magnesium as indicated.

## 💊 Management (search the current local/ADA protocol — this is the framework)

**1. Fluids first and foremost — this is the treatment.** **Isotonic saline (0.9% NaCl)** to restore circulating volume (e.g. ~1 L in the first hour, then titrate), replacing the large deficit **gradually over ~24–48 h** to avoid rapid osmotic shifts. **Switch to 0.45% saline** if the corrected sodium is high/normal once volume is restored; **add dextrose** when glucose falls to ~250–300 mg/dL to allow continued insulin without hypoglycaemia. **Aim to lower osmolality slowly** (avoid rapid falls → cerebral oedema).

**2. Potassium before and during insulin.** Total-body K⁺ is depleted; **insulin drives K⁺ into cells.** Replace K⁺ once it is < ~5.0–5.3 and the patient is passing urine; **hold insulin if K⁺ < 3.3** until replaced.

**3. Insulin — after fluids, low and slow.** Much of the glucose fall in HHS comes from **rehydration alone**, so start **insulin after initial fluid resuscitation** (often after the first hour), typically a **low-rate infusion (~0.05 U/kg/h)**; aim for a **gradual glucose decline (~50–75 mg/dL/h)** — too-rapid correction risks cerebral oedema and cardiovascular collapse.

**4. Treat the precipitant and prevent complications.** Antibiotics for infection, manage the trigger; **VTE prophylaxis** (high thrombotic risk from hyperviscosity); monitor for cerebral oedema, and transition to subcutaneous insulin only once the patient is eating and the hyperosmolar state has resolved.

## 🇹🇭 Thailand availability

Insulin, isotonic/half-isotonic saline, potassium, and dextrose are all standard on Thai wards. HHS typically presents in **elderly type 2 patients with infection** (common in Thai practice), so an infection screen and comorbidity management are integral. Management follows the ADA/international hyperglycaemic-crisis framework; the **Thai DM CPG (2566)** on disk covers diabetes management context.

> [!danger] 🚨 Red flags / strict avoidance
> - **Fluids are the priority; insulin is secondary and given AFTER initial fluid resuscitation, low-rate.**
> - **Correct glucose and osmolality slowly** — rapid falls risk cerebral oedema.
> - **Potassium falls with insulin** — replace early, and **hold insulin if K⁺ < 3.3**.
> - **Always find the precipitant** (infection, MI) — it drives the high mortality.
> - **VTE prophylaxis** — HHS is highly thrombogenic.

> [!tip] 🎯 High-Yield Recall
> - **HHS = glucose > 600 + effective osm > 320 + pH > 7.3/HCO₃ > 18 + minimal ketones + ↓consciousness.**
> - **Fluid first (0.9% NaCl), replace ~8–12 L deficit over 24–48 h**; add dextrose at glucose ~250–300; slow correction.
> - **Insulin AFTER fluids, ~0.05 U/kg/h**; K⁺ replacement (hold insulin if K⁺ < 3.3).
> - **Higher mortality than DKA** (elderly, comorbid); find the precipitant; **VTE prophylaxis**.
> - Contrast with [[DKA (Diabetic Ketoacidosis)|DKA]]: HHS has enough insulin to block ketogenesis → hyperosmolar not acidotic.
