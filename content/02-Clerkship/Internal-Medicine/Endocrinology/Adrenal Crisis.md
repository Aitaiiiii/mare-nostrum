# Adrenal Crisis (Acute Adrenal Insufficiency)

An acute, life-threatening deficiency of cortisol (± aldosterone) causing refractory hypotension and shock. It is **treated on suspicion** — the single most important action is **immediate parenteral hydrocortisone; you never wait for a confirmatory cortisol result**, because delay is fatal and the treatment is safe.

## 🧬 Causes & Pathophysiology

Cortisol maintains vascular tone (permits catecholamine action), gluconeogenesis, and the stress response; its loss → **vasodilatory/hypovolaemic shock**, hypoglycaemia, and inability to mount a stress response. Aldosterone loss (primary AI) adds salt wasting → hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia, volume depletion.

- **Primary (adrenal) — Addison's:** autoimmune (commonest in the West), **TB adrenalitis (important in Thailand)**, adrenal haemorrhage (Waterhouse–Friderichsen with meningococcaemia; anticoagulation), metastases, adrenoleukodystrophy → **cortisol AND aldosterone deficient** (hyperkalaemia, hyperpigmentation).
- **Secondary/tertiary (pituitary-hypothalamic):** **abrupt withdrawal of chronic glucocorticoids** (the commonest cause overall), pituitary disease/apoplexy, Sheehan's → cortisol deficient, aldosterone usually preserved.
- **Precipitant of a crisis** in a known/occult AI patient: **infection/sepsis, surgery, trauma, MI, vomiting (missed steroid doses), or failure to stress-dose.**

## 🩺 Clinical presentation

**Hypotension/shock poorly responsive to fluids and vasopressors** is the cardinal feature, with nausea/vomiting, abdominal pain (can mimic an acute abdomen), fever, weakness, confusion, and, if primary, **hyperpigmentation** and salt craving. Biochemistry: **hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia (primary), hypoglycaemia**, and sometimes hypercalcaemia. Suspect it strongly in shock with these electrolytes, in a patient on/recently off steroids, or with known AI + an intercurrent illness.

## 🩻 Investigations (do not delay treatment)

- **Draw a random serum cortisol (± ACTH, aldosterone, renin) BEFORE the first hydrocortisone dose if it takes seconds** — a low cortisol during stress confirms it; a **short Synacthen (ACTH-stimulation) test** is done later once stable (dexamethasone can be used acutely as it does not interfere with the cortisol assay if the test can't wait).
- Electrolytes, glucose, renal function, CBC, **septic screen** (find the precipitant), and cortisol/ACTH.
- **Never delay hydrocortisone for these results.**

## 💊 Management

**1. Hydrocortisone — immediately, empirically.** **Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV (or IM) bolus STAT**, then **200 mg per 24 h** by continuous IV infusion **or 50 mg IV/IM every 6 hours.** At these stress doses hydrocortisone provides sufficient mineralocorticoid effect, so **fludrocortisone is not needed acutely** (add later, when hydrocortisone < 50 mg/day, in primary AI). If the diagnosis is uncertain and you want to preserve the Synacthen test, dexamethasone 4 mg is an alternative first dose.

**2. Fluid resuscitation.** **Rapid IV isotonic saline — ~1000 mL in the first hour**, then further rehydration (often **4–6 L in 24 h**) titrated to haemodynamics, watching for overload in the elderly/renal-impaired. **Correct hypoglycaemia** (IV dextrose).

**3. Treat the precipitant and the electrolytes.** Find and treat **infection/sepsis** or other trigger; hyponatraemia and hyperkalaemia usually correct with hydrocortisone + saline (avoid overly rapid Na⁺ correction — see [[Hyponatremia]]). Monitor for the hyponatraemia over-correction risk as cortisol replacement drives a water diuresis.

**4. After stabilisation.** Transition to oral hydrocortisone with a taper to maintenance, add fludrocortisone in primary AI, and provide **sick-day rules education + an emergency hydrocortisone injection kit + a medical-alert bracelet** to prevent recurrence.

## 🇹🇭 Thailand availability

Hydrocortisone (and dexamethasone), IV saline, and dextrose are all on the Thai NLEM and stocked in Thai EDs. Two Thai-relevant points: **TB is an important cause of primary adrenal insufficiency** (bilateral adrenal involvement), and **glucocorticoid-induced AI is common** because of widespread use of steroids (including in unregulated compounded/"ยาชุด" preparations) — abrupt cessation or an intercurrent illness in such a patient can precipitate a crisis.

> [!danger] 🚨 Red flags / strict avoidance
> - **Give hydrocortisone 100 mg IV on suspicion — never wait for the cortisol result.**
> - **Shock refractory to fluids/pressors + hyponatraemia/hyperkalaemia/hypoglycaemia** = adrenal crisis until excluded.
> - **Steroid-dependent patients must be stress-dosed** during illness/surgery — failure to do so is a classic precipitant.
> - Treat the precipitant (usually **infection**) and correct hypoglycaemia.
> - Don't over-correct hyponatraemia once cortisol replacement starts a water diuresis (osmotic demyelination risk).

> [!tip] 🎯 High-Yield Recall
> - **Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV STAT, then 200 mg/24 h infusion (or 50 mg q6h)** — on suspicion, don't wait.
> - **Isotonic saline ~1 L in the first hour** (4–6 L/24 h) + **treat hypoglycaemia** + treat the precipitant.
> - Primary AI = cortisol **+** aldosterone deficient → hyponatraemia + **hyperkalaemia** + hyperpigmentation; TB is a Thai cause.
> - Commonest cause overall = **abrupt glucocorticoid withdrawal**; stress-dose steroid-dependent patients.
> - Fludrocortisone not needed at stress-dose hydrocortisone; add on step-down; **sick-day rules + emergency kit** at discharge.
