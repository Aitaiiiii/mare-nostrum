# Rhabdomyolysis

Breakdown of skeletal muscle releasing intracellular contents (**myoglobin, potassium, phosphate, CK, urate**) into the circulation. The danger is not the muscle injury itself but its systemic consequences — **acute kidney injury, hyperkalaemia (can arrest the heart), and, in crush injury, hypocalcaemia** — all of which are largely preventable with **early, aggressive fluid resuscitation.**

## 🧬 Causes & Pathophysiology

Myocyte membrane failure → Ca²⁺ influx, activation of proteases, and cell lysis. Released **myoglobin causes AKI** by three mechanisms: renal vasoconstriction, direct tubular toxicity, and tubular obstruction by myoglobin casts (worse in acid urine and hypovolaemia). Causes:

- **Trauma/compression:** crush injury, prolonged immobilisation ("found down"), compartment syndrome.
- **Exertion/hyperthermia:** extreme exercise, seizures/[[Status Epilepticus]], [[Heat Stroke]], neuroleptic malignant/serotonin syndrome, agitation.
- **Toxins/drugs:** alcohol, **statins** (esp. with interacting drugs), cocaine/amphetamines, [[Organophosphate Poisoning|organophosphates]].
- **Ischaemia:** [[Acute Limb Ischemia|acute limb ischaemia]]/reperfusion, prolonged tourniquet.
- **Infections, electrolyte disturbance (hypokalaemia, hypophosphataemia), and inherited myopathies.**

## 🩺 Clinical presentation

Classic triad **muscle pain, weakness, and dark ("tea-/cola-coloured") urine** — but many have few muscle symptoms. Look for swollen tender muscles, a compression history, and the complications: arrhythmia (hyperkalaemia), oliguria (AKI), and, in crush, tetany/arrhythmia (hypocalcaemia). **Compartment syndrome** may be cause or consequence.

## 🩻 Investigations

- **Serum creatine kinase (CK)** — the diagnostic marker; **> 5× ULN (or > 1000 IU/L)** defines significant rhabdomyolysis and triggers treatment; peaks at 24–72 h, and the peak roughly tracks AKI risk (higher risk when > 5000, especially > 15,000–20,000).
- **Urinalysis:** dipstick **positive for "blood" but few/no RBCs on microscopy = myoglobinuria** (the classic clue).
- **Electrolytes: potassium (↑, urgent), phosphate (↑), calcium (↓ early, ↑ later), urate (↑)**, bicarbonate/venous gas, renal function, and coagulation (DIC in severe cases).
- ECG for hyperkalaemia; CK trend to guide fluid duration.

## 💊 Management

**1. Early, aggressive isotonic fluid resuscitation — the cornerstone.** Start **as soon as rhabdomyolysis is suspected** (before AKI develops). **Isotonic saline (or Ringer's lactate), begin ~400 mL/h (large volumes, often 10–12 L/day), titrated to a urine output of 1–3 mL/kg/h (up to ~300 mL/h)** with close monitoring for overload. Early volume dilutes and flushes myoglobin and restores renal perfusion. **Crush syndrome:** begin fluids **before/at extrication** and target UOP 200–300 mL/h.

**2. Do NOT routinely alkalinise or give mannitol.** **Urinary alkalinisation (bicarbonate) is not routinely recommended** — consider only case-by-case if urine pH < 6.5 with adequate output; mannitol has no proven benefit. Isotonic-fluid volume is what matters.

**3. Treat the metabolic emergencies.**
- **Hyperkalaemia** — the acute killer: ECG, calcium (membrane), insulin-dextrose + salbutamol (shift), removal (binder/diuretic/dialysis) — see [[Dyskalemia (Hypo- & Hyperkalemia)]].
- **Hypocalcaemia** — usually asymptomatic; **treat only if symptomatic or with severe hyperkalaemia** (calcium is sequestered in muscle and rebounds → over-treatment causes later hypercalcaemia).
- **Hyperphosphataemia** and hyperuricaemia — supportive.

**4. AKI and dialysis.** Manage as [[Acute Kidney Injury|AKI]]; **renal replacement therapy for the usual AEIOU indications** (refractory hyperkalaemia, acidosis, volume overload, uraemia). Standard dialysis clears myoglobin poorly, so RRT is for the complications, not to "remove myoglobin".

**5. Treat the cause + compartment syndrome.** Stop the offending drug, cool a hyperthermic patient, treat seizures/agitation; **fasciotomy for compartment syndrome.**

## 🇹🇭 Thailand availability

Isotonic fluids, potassium-lowering agents, calcium, and dialysis are all standard in Thai hospitals. Thai-relevant causes worth flagging: **heat stroke (hot-season/exertional), snake envenomation (some cause myotoxicity), prolonged "found-down" from alcohol, seizures, and strenuous unaccustomed exertion** — all common ER contexts where a raised CK and dark urine should prompt early fluids.

> [!danger] 🚨 Red flags / strict avoidance
> - **Start aggressive isotonic fluids early** (before AKI) and target UOP 1–3 mL/kg/h — this is the whole game.
> - **Hyperkalaemia can cause cardiac arrest** — ECG and treat urgently; it is disproportionate to the creatinine.
> - **Dipstick "blood" with no RBCs = myoglobinuria** — recognise it.
> - **Don't over-treat hypocalcaemia** (rebound hypercalcaemia); treat only if symptomatic/severe hyperkalaemia.
> - **Bicarbonate/mannitol are NOT routine**; watch for and decompress **compartment syndrome**.

> [!tip] 🎯 High-Yield Recall
> - **CK > 5× ULN (or > 1000)** = rhabdomyolysis; **dipstick blood + no RBCs = myoglobinuria**; risk of AKI rises with CK (esp. > 5000).
> - **Early aggressive isotonic saline/LR ~400 mL/h → UOP 1–3 mL/kg/h** (crush: 200–300 mL/h) — the cornerstone.
> - **Bicarbonate/mannitol not routine**; treat **hyperkalaemia** hard; treat **hypocalcaemia only if symptomatic**.
> - Dialyse for **AEIOU**; standard HD does not remove myoglobin well.
> - Find the cause (crush, statin, heat/exertion, seizure, toxin); **fasciotomy** for compartment syndrome.
