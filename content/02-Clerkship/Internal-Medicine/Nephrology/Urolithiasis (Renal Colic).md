# Urolithiasis (Renal Colic)

Calculi in the urinary tract; the ER presentation is **acute ureteric colic** from a stone obstructing the ureter. Most stones pass spontaneously, so the job is **effective analgesia, the right imaging, and identifying the two situations that cannot wait — an obstructed infected kidney (urosepsis) and obstruction of a solitary/transplant kidney or bilateral obstruction with AKI.**

## 🧬 Stone types & Pathophysiology

Colic arises from acute ureteric obstruction → ↑ intraluminal pressure, capsular stretch, and prostaglandin-mediated pain; the classic sites of impaction are the three ureteric narrowings (pelvi-ureteric junction, pelvic brim/iliac vessels, vesico-ureteric junction). Stone composition: **calcium oxalate (most common)**, calcium phosphate, **uric acid** (radiolucent, forms in acid urine — the only type amenable to dissolution by urinary alkalinisation), **struvite** (magnesium ammonium phosphate — infection stones with urease organisms, form staghorn calculi), and **cystine** (hereditary).

## 🩺 Clinical presentation

Sudden, severe, **colicky flank pain radiating to the groin/testis/labia**, with the patient restless and unable to lie still (unlike peritonitis), nausea/vomiting, and **haematuria** (micro- or macroscopic). A VUJ stone gives lower urinary tract symptoms (frequency, urgency). **Fever/rigors + colic = obstructed infected system until proven otherwise** (urological emergency).

## 🩻 Investigations

- **Low-dose non-contrast CT-KUB is first-line** (sensitivity/specificity > 95%; sizes and locates the stone, shows hydronephrosis) — image within 24 h. **US** is first-line in **pregnancy** and young patients (radiation-sparing) and shows hydronephrosis.
- **Urinalysis** (haematuria, and crucially nitrite/leukocytes → infection), **urine culture**; **CBC, electrolytes, creatinine, calcium, urate**; pregnancy test.
- Stone analysis on any passed/retrieved stone; metabolic work-up for recurrent stone formers.

## 💊 Management

**1. Analgesia — NSAID first-line.** **NSAIDs (e.g. diclofenac IM/IV/PR, ketorolac) are more effective than opioids** for renal colic and reduce the need for rescue analgesia — give an NSAID first (unless contraindicated: AKI, bleeding, pregnancy) and add an **opioid** for breakthrough. Antiemetic as needed.

**2. Decide: pass or intervene (by size, symptoms, and obstruction).**
- **Stones ≤ 5 mm** usually pass spontaneously → analgesia + hydration + watchful waiting.
- **Stones ≤ 10 mm (distal/mid ureter), controlled pain, no infection → Medical Expulsive Therapy (MET) with an α-blocker (tamsulosin 0.4 mg PO OD) for up to ~30 days** to aid passage; strain urine, follow up.
- **Stones > 10 mm, failed passage, or intractable pain → urological intervention** (ureteroscopy + laser, ESWL, or PCNL for large/staghorn stones).

**3. The emergencies — obstruction that must be decompressed now.**
- **Obstructed + infected kidney (fever/sepsis + obstructing stone)** → **urgent decompression (ureteric stent or percutaneous nephrostomy) + IV antibiotics** — do NOT attempt definitive stone removal in a septic obstructed system.
- **Obstruction of a solitary or transplant kidney, bilateral obstruction, or AKI** → urgent drainage.
- Refer these to urology immediately.

**4. Prevention (recurrent formers):** high fluid intake (urine output > 2–2.5 L/day), dietary modification (normal calcium, reduce sodium/oxalate/animal protein), **potassium citrate** for calcium/uric-acid stones, **urinary alkalinisation (dissolves uric acid stones)**, thiazide for hypercalciuria, allopurinol for hyperuricosuria.

## 🇹🇭 Thailand availability & context

NSAIDs, α-blockers (tamsulosin), potassium citrate, allopurinol, antibiotics, and both stent/nephrostomy and ureteroscopy/ESWL/PCNL are available at Thai hospitals. Thailand's **north-east has a very high prevalence of urolithiasis** (dietary and climatic factors, dehydration, bladder stones historically), so renal/ureteric stones are a frequent Thai ER presentation — and **urease-producing infection (struvite/staghorn) stones** with recurrent UTI are common.

> [!danger] 🚨 Red flags / strict avoidance
> - **Fever + obstructing stone = obstructed infected kidney → EMERGENCY decompression** (stent/nephrostomy) + antibiotics; do not just give analgesia.
> - **Solitary/transplant kidney, bilateral obstruction, or AKI** → urgent drainage.
> - **Avoid NSAIDs** if AKI, active bleeding, or pregnancy — use opioids/paracetamol there.
> - A first "renal colic" in an **older patient** — exclude a **leaking AAA** mimicking colic before anchoring.

> [!tip] 🎯 High-Yield Recall
> - **Low-dose non-contrast CT-KUB** first-line (US in pregnancy); **NSAID first-line analgesia** (> opioids).
> - **≤ 5 mm** pass; **≤ 10 mm distal → MET tamsulosin 0.4 mg OD ×30 d**; **> 10 mm / failed → urology** (URS/ESWL/PCNL).
> - **Obstructed + infected = decompress urgently** — the must-not-miss.
> - **Uric acid stones** are radiolucent and **dissolve with urinary alkalinisation**; struvite = infection/staghorn.
> - Prevent: fluids > 2.5 L/day, citrate, thiazide (hypercalciuria), allopurinol (hyperuricosuria).

> [!info]- 🔍 Verification status
> ✅ Searched & verified 2026-09-18 against **AUA (2025/2026) surgical stone guideline**, **EAU Urolithiasis 2025**, and **NICE NG118**: low-dose non-contrast CT-KUB first-line; NSAIDs superior to opioids; MET with α-blockers for ≤ 10 mm ureteric stones ~30 days; **urgent renal drainage for obstructing stone + suspected infection**.
> ⚠️ Spontaneous-passage rates by size are approximate; MET benefit is greatest for distal ureteric stones.
