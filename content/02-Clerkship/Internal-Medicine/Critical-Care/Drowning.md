# Drowning

The process of respiratory impairment from submersion/immersion in liquid — a leading cause of accidental death, especially in children. The unifying pathology is **hypoxia**, so management is fundamentally about **ventilation and oxygenation, starting with rescue breaths**; the outdated "wet vs dry" and "fresh vs salt water" distinctions do not change acute care.

## 🧬 Pathophysiology

Submersion → breath-holding → involuntary aspiration/laryngospasm → **hypoxaemia**, which drives the cardiac arrest (a **hypoxic, not primarily cardiac, arrest** — hence airway/breathing come first). Aspirated water washes out surfactant and injures the alveolar-capillary membrane → **pulmonary oedema/ARDS and secondary hypoxaemia** over hours. Hypoxic-ischaemic brain injury determines neurological outcome; **hypothermia** frequently coexists (and can be protective — see [[Accidental Hypothermia]]).

## 🩺 Presentation

Ranges from a coughing, mildly breathless patient to respiratory failure, coma, and cardiac arrest. Look for **hypoxaemia, crackles/wheeze, altered mental status, hypothermia**, and complications (aspiration, arrhythmia, AKI, delayed pulmonary oedema). A relevant **precipitant** may exist (seizure, syncope, arrhythmia — e.g. long-QT/channelopathy, alcohol, trauma, hypoglycaemia) — consider what caused the submersion.

## 💊 Management

**1. Ventilation/oxygenation FIRST — it is a hypoxic arrest.** In-water/on-scene **rescue breaths** are the priority; start with **airway and breathing (5 rescue breaths before compressions in drowning arrest)** and give high-flow oxygen. **Do NOT perform abdominal thrusts to "remove water"** (ineffective, causes aspiration/vomiting). Suction as needed; intubate/ventilate for respiratory failure or coma; use **PEEP/lung-protective ventilation** for the surfactant-depleted lung.

**2. Cardiac arrest.** Standard [[CPR - ACLS|resuscitation]] with an emphasis on oxygenation/ventilation; **prolonged resuscitation is justified in hypothermic drowning** ("not dead until warm and dead"); consider ECLS in cold-water arrest.

**3. C-spine — do NOT routinely immobilise.** Routine cervical-spine immobilisation is **not recommended** in drowning (low injury rate, hinders airway) — reserve it for a **suggestive mechanism** (diving, high-impact, signs of trauma, intoxication with a fall).

**4. Supportive + observation.** Rewarm ([[Accidental Hypothermia|hypothermia]]), correct hypoglycaemia/electrolytes, treat bronchospasm, and manage evolving **ARDS**. **No prophylactic antibiotics/steroids.** **Observation:** a patient who is **asymptomatic with a normal respiratory exam and normal SpO₂ after ~4–6 hours of observation** may be safely discharged; any symptoms/hypoxaemia → admit and monitor for delayed pulmonary injury.

**Prevention** (public-health emphasis): supervision, barriers/fencing, swimming skills, life jackets — drowning is highly preventable.

## 🇹🇭 Thailand availability

Oxygen, ventilators, warming, and ICU care are available; drowning is a **major cause of childhood death in Thailand** (canals, ponds, rivers, the sea, and seasonal flooding), so recognition, ventilation-first resuscitation, and the observation rule are high-yield, alongside strong **prevention** messaging. Consider a cardiac/seizure precipitant in unexplained adult drowning.

> [!danger] 🚨 Red flags / strict avoidance
> - **Hypoxia is the problem — ventilate/oxygenate first** (rescue breaths before compressions in drowning arrest).
> - **No abdominal thrusts** to "remove water" (harmful).
> - **Do NOT routinely immobilise the c-spine** — only for a suggestive mechanism.
> - **Delayed pulmonary injury/ARDS** develops over hours → observe symptomatic patients; asymptomatic + normal SpO₂/exam after ~4–6 h can go home.
> - Look for a **precipitant** (seizure, arrhythmia/long-QT, hypoglycaemia, alcohol, trauma).
