# Anaphylaxis

A severe, rapidly-evolving, potentially fatal systemic hypersensitivity reaction. It is a **clinical diagnosis treated by reflex** — **intramuscular adrenaline is the first and only first-line drug**, given the moment anaphylaxis is recognised. Delayed adrenaline is the dominant modifiable cause of death; antihistamines and corticosteroids are adjuncts that never substitute for it.

## 🧬 Mechanism & triggers

Most anaphylaxis is **IgE-mediated** mast-cell/basophil degranulation → histamine, tryptase, prostaglandins, leukotrienes → vasodilation and capillary leak (distributive shock), bronchoconstriction, laryngeal oedema, and increased secretions. Non-IgE ("anaphylactoid", e.g. radiocontrast, NSAIDs, direct mast-cell activators) produces an identical picture and identical treatment. Common triggers: **foods** (nuts, shellfish, egg, milk), **drugs** (β-lactams, NSAIDs, contrast, perioperative agents — neuromuscular blockers), **insect stings** (Hymenoptera), and latex; **idiopathic** in a minority. **Cofactors** (exercise, alcohol, NSAIDs, infection) lower the threshold.

## 🩺 Clinical presentation & diagnostic criteria

Onset is usually minutes to 1–2 hours after exposure; **the faster the onset, the more severe.** Skin/mucosal signs (urticaria, flushing, angio-oedema) are present in ~80–90% **but their absence does not exclude anaphylaxis** — up to 10–20% have none, especially drug/venom reactions presenting as sudden hypotension or bronchospasm.

**Diagnose anaphylaxis if EITHER (do not wait to confirm before treating):**
1. **Acute onset** (minutes–hours) of **skin/mucosal involvement** (hives, itch, swollen lips/tongue/uvula) **PLUS at least one of:** respiratory compromise (dyspnoea, wheeze, stridor, hypoxaemia), reduced BP/end-organ hypoperfusion, or severe GI symptoms (crampy pain, repetitive vomiting).
2. **Acute onset of hypotension, bronchospasm, or laryngeal involvement** after exposure to a **known or highly probable allergen for that patient**, even **without** skin signs (WAO 2-criteria simplification).

Danger features: **stridor, hoarseness, drooling, tongue/pharyngeal swelling** (impending airway loss) and **hypotension/collapse** (anaphylactic shock). **Serum tryptase** (drawn 1–3 h from onset, with a baseline later) supports the diagnosis retrospectively but must never delay treatment.

## 🗂️ Differential diagnosis

Vasovagal syncope (bradycardia, pallor, no urticaria/bronchospasm), acute asthma without systemic features, [[Asthma Exacerbation]], panic/anxiety with hyperventilation, scombroid poisoning (histamine in spoiled fish — mimics but food-borne), hereditary angio-oedema (no urticaria, C1-INH, unresponsive to adrenaline), and other causes of shock.

## 💊 Management

**1. Adrenaline (epinephrine) IM — immediately, first-line, no adjunct precedes it.**
- **0.5 mg IM (0.01 mg/kg, max 0.5 mg in adults) using the 1 mg/mL (1:1000) solution, into the anterolateral thigh (vastus lateralis).**
- **Repeat every 5–15 minutes if no improvement** — most deaths follow delayed or omitted adrenaline. Needing ≥ 2 doses defines **refractory** anaphylaxis and predicts a biphasic course.
- **Position:** lay the patient **supine with legs elevated** (or left-lateral if pregnant/vomiting); **do not sit or stand a hypotensive patient up** — sudden upright posture during anaphylactic shock ("empty ventricle syndrome") can precipitate fatal cardiac arrest.

**2. Airway, breathing, circulation.**
- High-flow **oxygen**; prepare for **early intubation** if stridor/airway oedema — a difficult airway that only worsens with delay.
- **Large-bore IV access + rapid crystalloid bolus** (e.g. 1–2 L, 10–20 mL/kg, repeat to response) for hypotension — the distributive shock is fluid-responsive.

**3. Refractory anaphylaxis (persisting after ≥ 2 IM doses):**
- **IV adrenaline infusion** titrated to effect (senior/monitored setting) — do not give IV push boluses on the ward (arrhythmia/ischaemia risk).
- **Patients on β-blockers** with a poor adrenaline response → **glucagon 1–5 mg IV** then infusion (bypasses β-blockade).
- Add a second vasopressor (see [[Inotrope & Vasopressor]]) for refractory shock; ICU.

**4. Adjuncts — second-line, AFTER adrenaline, never instead of it:**
- **H1-antihistamine** (e.g. chlorpheniramine/diphenhydramine) relieves **cutaneous** itch/urticaria only — no effect on airway or shock.
- **Corticosteroid** (hydrocortisone/prednisolone) — routine use is **no longer recommended** to prevent biphasic reactions (evidence does not support it); may be used case-by-case, e.g. concurrent asthma. Do not prioritise it.
- **Inhaled SABA** (salbutamol) for persistent bronchospasm as an add-on.

**5. Observation & discharge.**
- **Observe** after resolution for a **biphasic reaction**; duration is individualised — **shorter (~1–2 h) for a mild, single-dose, promptly-treated reaction; longer (6–12+ h or admit) for severe reactions, ≥ 2 adrenaline doses, airway involvement, poor access to care, or prior biphasic history.**
- **On discharge: prescribe TWO adrenaline autoinjectors + train use, a written anaphylaxis action plan, trigger avoidance, and refer to an allergist/immunologist.** Consider stopping/switching any β-blocker/ACE-inhibitor where feasible.

## 🇹🇭 Thailand availability

Adrenaline ampoules (1 mg/mL), IV crystalloid, chlorpheniramine, hydrocortisone, salbutamol, and glucagon are all standard and on the Thai NLEM. **Adrenaline auto-injectors (EpiPen) are not consistently available/reimbursed in Thailand** — many patients are instead prescribed **pre-filled adrenaline syringes/ampoules with an administration plan**, so discharge counselling must cover the device the patient can actually obtain. Anaphylaxis is managed the same way across Thai EDs (Thai allergy/immunology society guidance mirrors the international parameter).

> [!danger] 🚨 Red flags / strict avoidance
> - **Give IM adrenaline FIRST and EARLY** — do not delay for antihistamines, steroids, or to confirm the diagnosis.
> - **Do NOT sit up or stand a hypotensive patient** — keep supine with legs raised (empty-ventricle arrest risk).
> - **Absence of urticaria does NOT exclude anaphylaxis** — sudden hypotension/bronchospasm after a trigger is enough.
> - **Antihistamines and steroids are adjuncts** — they do not treat airway or shock and do not reliably prevent biphasic reactions.
> - β-blocked + refractory → **glucagon**; avoid IV adrenaline *push* on the ward (use an infusion).

> [!tip] 🎯 High-Yield Recall
> - **Adrenaline 0.5 mg IM anterolateral thigh (1:1000), repeat q5–15 min** — first-line, reflexive.
> - **Supine + legs up**, high-flow O₂, large-bore IV + fluid bolus; early airway if stridor.
> - **Refractory (≥ 2 doses) → IV adrenaline infusion**; on β-blocker → **glucagon**.
> - Adjuncts (antihistamine, steroid, SABA) are **second-line**; steroids no longer routine for biphasic prevention.
> - Discharge: **2 autoinjectors/pre-filled adrenaline + action plan + allergist referral**; observe longer if severe/biphasic risk.
