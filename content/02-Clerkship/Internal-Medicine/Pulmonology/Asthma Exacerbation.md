# Asthma Exacerbation (Acute Severe Asthma)

An asthma exacerbation ("flare-up", "attack", "acute severe asthma") is an acute or subacute worsening of symptoms and lung function beyond the patient's usual state, driven by increased airway inflammation, bronchoconstriction, and mucus plugging. This page covers the **acute ER/ward event** in adults and adolescents; the chronic disease, stepwise controller therapy, and severe/biologic asthma live on the future [[Asthma]] page (seeded below). The single decision that changes outcome here is speed: repeated bronchodilator + **early systemic corticosteroid** within the first hour, with severity re-assessed at 1 hour to escalate or discharge.

## 🧬 Triggers & Pathophysiology

The airway obstruction of an attack is three overlapping processes on a background of type-2 (usually eosinophilic) or, less often, non-type-2 inflammation: **acute bronchial smooth-muscle constriction** (rapidly reversible with a β2-agonist), **airway wall oedema and inflammatory infiltration** (reversed over hours-to-days by corticosteroid), and **mucus/mucous-plug formation** (slowest to clear, the dominant lesion in fatal asthma). Expiratory airflow limitation causes dynamic hyperinflation and intrinsic PEEP, raising the work of breathing; V/Q mismatch produces hypoxaemia. A **rising or normal PaCO₂ in a breathless asthmatic is an ominous sign** — during an attack the patient hyperventilates and PaCO₂ should be low, so a "normal" (≥40 mmHg) or rising CO₂ signals fatigue and impending respiratory failure, not improvement.

Common precipitants: viral URTI (rhinovirus most often), aeroallergen exposure, poor controller adherence or reliance on SABA alone, air pollution/smoke, NSAID/aspirin (AERD phenotype), β-blockers, and exercise/cold air. Ask specifically about **risk factors for asthma-related death**, which flag a patient for aggressive treatment and a low admission threshold.

> [!note] 💎 Pearls — risk factors for near-fatal/fatal asthma
> A history of **intubation or ICU admission** for asthma · a **severe exacerbation in the last 12 months** · **≥3 SABA canisters/year** (over-use) or **no ICS** on the regimen · poor adherence or no written action plan · food allergy in a patient with asthma · psychiatric disease/social problems. Any of these = treat the current attack as high-risk regardless of how it looks.

## 🩺 Clinical presentation & severity triage

Assess severity from **degree of dyspnoea, ability to speak, respiratory rate, pulse, oxygen saturation, and lung function (PEF)** while simultaneously starting SABA and oxygen — do not delay treatment to complete the assessment. GINA collapses the practical triage into two actionable tiers plus a "life-threatening" alarm state that bypasses stepwise care.

| Feature | Mild–Moderate | Severe | Life-threatening |
|---|---|---|---|
| Talks in | sentences/phrases | words | — |
| Mental state | not agitated | agitated | drowsy, confused |
| Accessory muscles | not used | used | paradoxical thoraco-abdominal |
| Respiratory rate | increased | > 30 /min | — |
| Pulse | 100–120 bpm | > 120 bpm | bradycardia |
| SpO₂ (room air) | 90–95% | < 90% | < 90% + cyanosis |
| PEF (% predicted/best) | > 50% | ≤ 50% | ≤ 50% or unrecordable |
| Chest | wheeze | loud wheeze | **silent chest** |

**Silent chest, cyanosis, drowsiness/confusion, "air hunger", bradycardia, or exhaustion define life-threatening asthma** → treat as severe, call for senior/ICU help, and prepare for intubation. A normalising or rising PaCO₂ on ABG in this context is a further alarm. Anaphylaxis can present as acute bronchospasm with wheeze — if there is urticaria, angio-oedema, hypotension, or a known trigger, treat as [[Anaphylaxis]] with IM adrenaline first.

## 🩻 Investigations

Clinical assessment and **serial PEF/FEV₁** drive management; investigations are adjuncts and must never delay bronchodilator/steroid.

- **PEF or FEV₁** — measure before treatment if feasible and again after 1 hour. Response ≥ 60% predicted (or personal best) at 1 hour predicts safe discharge; failure to reach 60% predicts admission.
- **SpO₂ / ABG** — SpO₂ continuously. ABG only if SpO₂ < 90–92% on treatment, severe/life-threatening features, or deterioration. Look for a **normal-or-high PaCO₂** (impending failure) and metabolic acidosis (lactataemia from β2-agonist/work of breathing).
- **Chest X-ray — not routine.** Order only to exclude a complication or mimic: **pneumothorax/pneumomediastinum**, consolidation, or when the diagnosis is in doubt or the patient is not improving.
- **Do not routinely give antibiotics** — most attacks are viral; reserve for clear evidence of bacterial infection (fever + purulent sputum + focal consolidation).
- Bloods (CBC, electrolytes) as clinically indicated; watch **potassium** (β2-agonists + steroids drive hypokalaemia). Consider blood/serum theophylline level only if the patient takes it.

## 🗂️ Differential diagnosis

Wheeze and acute dyspnoea are not always asthma — reconsider in the atypical or non-responding patient:

| Mimicker | Discriminator |
|---|---|
| [[COPD Exacerbation]] | older smoker, chronic sputum, less reversibility, chronic CO₂ retention |
| Acute pulmonary oedema ([[ADHF]]) | "cardiac asthma", orthopnoea, basal crackles, ↑BNP, CXR congestion |
| [[Anaphylaxis]] | urticaria/angio-oedema, hypotension, GI symptoms, known trigger — needs IM adrenaline |
| [[VTE (DVT & Pulmonary Embolism)\|Pulmonary embolism]] | pleuritic pain, clear chest, hypoxaemia out of proportion, risk factors |
| Inducible laryngeal obstruction (VCD) | inspiratory stridor, normal SpO₂, flattened inspiratory flow-volume loop, poor steroid response |
| Foreign body / tracheal obstruction | focal/monophonic wheeze, sudden onset, localising history |
| Pneumothorax | sudden pleuritic pain, unilateral absent breath sounds — see [[Pneumothorax]] |

## 💊 Management

The ER bundle is **oxygen + repeated SABA + early systemic corticosteroid**, with **ipratropium and IV magnesium reserved for severe** disease, then a structured 1-hour reassessment. Every dose below is searched against GINA 2025 and TAC (Thai Asthma Council) 2025.

**1. Oxygen — controlled, titrated.** Target **SpO₂ 93–95%** in adults (avoid over-oxygenation, which can worsen hypercapnia). Deliver by nasal cannula or mask; life-threatening hypoxaemia refractory to this needs escalation, not just higher FiO₂.

**2. Inhaled SABA (salbutamol) — the first and repeated drug.** **4–10 puffs by pMDI + spacer, or 2.5 mg by nebuliser, repeated every 20 minutes for the first hour** (three doses). pMDI-plus-spacer is at least as effective as nebuliser in most patients and is preferred where feasible; use an **oxygen-driven nebuliser** (2.5 mg salbutamol in 3 mL NSS) for severe disease. After the first hour, titrate frequency to response. Continuous nebulisation is an option in severe/refractory cases.

**3. Ipratropium bromide — add for severe (or moderate not responding).** **500 mcg (or 4 puffs of 20 mcg) by nebuliser/pMDI+spacer every 20 minutes for the first hour**, combined with SABA. Adding ipratropium to SABA in severe exacerbations reduces admissions and improves lung function; discontinue once the patient reaches the ward — it adds little beyond the acute phase.

**4. Systemic corticosteroid — give early to every exacerbation needing more than a single SABA dose.** Speeds resolution and prevents relapse; benefit begins within ~4 hours, so give within the first hour.
- **Prednisolone 40–50 mg PO once daily** (TAC: 30–50 mg/day) — oral is as effective as IV when the patient can swallow.
- **Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV q6h** or **methylprednisolone** if vomiting, unable to swallow, or too breathless.
- **Dexamethasone** (e.g. 12–16 mg) is an alternative but **limit to 1–2 days** (long half-life) and switch to prednisolone if symptoms relapse.
- **Duration: 5–7 days in adults**, no taper needed for a short course; **3–5 days in children**. A course longer than this does not add benefit.

**5. IV magnesium sulfate — for severe/life-threatening not responding to initial therapy.** **A single 2 g IV infusion over 20 minutes** in adults (paediatric 40–50 mg/kg, max 2 g), considered when the patient has severe features or **PEF < 60% predicted after 1 hour** of intensive bronchodilator + steroid. It reduces hospital admissions in this subgroup. **Nebulised magnesium is NO longer recommended (GINA 2025).**

**6. Second-line / escalation for refractory severe asthma.** Consider high-flow nasal oxygen or a trial of NIV in selected patients (senior-led; NIV in asthma is not firmly evidence-based and must not delay intubation). **Add-on high-dose ICS nebulisation** is offered in the Thai (TAC) severe-exacerbation pathway. IV β2-agonist and IV aminophylline are largely obsolete (toxicity ≫ benefit) — reserve for specialist/ICU decisions. **Intubation is a clinical decision** (exhaustion, silent chest, falling GCS, rising PaCO₂, refractory hypoxaemia), not a number; ketamine is a favoured induction agent for its bronchodilator effect, and ventilation uses a **low respiratory rate, permissive hypercapnia, and long expiratory time** to avoid dynamic hyperinflation and barotrauma.

**7. Reassess at 1 hour and decide disposition.**
- **Good response** (symptoms improved, stable vitals, PEF ≥ 60–80% predicted, SpO₂ ≥ 94% on air) → observe, then **discharge** on the plan below.
- **Incomplete/poor response** (PEF < 60%, persistent symptoms, unstable) → continue intensive treatment, admit; life-threatening features or ABC compromise → ICU + intubation.

**8. Discharge management — the switch that prevents the next attack.**
- **Start or continue an ICS-containing regimen before the patient leaves — never discharge on SABA alone.** For a patient previously on SABA-only, switch to **ICS–formoterol maintenance-and-reliever (MART)** or start medium-dose ICS-LABA; a patient already on ICS-formoterol resumes/steps up MART.
- **Complete the OCS course** (prednisolone 40–50 mg/day to 5–7 days total).
- **Review inhaler technique and adherence, provide a written action plan, and arrange follow-up within 2–7 days.** Identify and address the trigger.

## 🇹🇭 Thailand availability & Thai CPG (TAC 2025 vs GINA 2025)

**All acute-phase drugs are on the Thai NLEM and universally available**: salbutamol (pMDI/neb), ipratropium bromide, prednisolone, hydrocortisone, dexamethasone, and magnesium sulfate. The **Thai Asthma Council (TAC) 2025** ER pathway aligns closely with GINA 2025: severity triaged into mild–moderate vs severe, treat-then-reassess-at-1-hour, oxygen to **SpO₂ 93–95%**, repeated RABA (± ipratropium for severe) every 20 min for 1 hour, and **prednisolone 30–50 mg/day for ≤ 5 days**. Two Thai-flavoured points: TAC explicitly lists **dexamethasone (including IV) as a systemic-steroid option** in the acute pathway, and it offers **add-on high-dose ICS nebulisation** for the severe non-responder — reflecting Thai practice and the local nebulised-budesonide consensus. GINA and TAC agree on IV magnesium 2 g for severe non-responders and on **dropping nebulised magnesium**. Neither recommends routine antibiotics or routine CXR.

> [!danger] 🚨 Red flags / strict avoidance
> - **Silent chest, cyanosis, drowsiness/confusion, or a rising/normal PaCO₂** = life-threatening → senior/ICU, prepare to intubate. Do not be reassured by a "quiet" chest or a "normal" CO₂.
> - **Never discharge on SABA-only** — every exacerbation needs ICS-containing therapy started before discharge; SABA-only treatment increases the risk of death.
> - **Do not withhold or delay systemic corticosteroid** — give within the first hour to any attack beyond a single SABA dose.
> - Do not rely on β-blockers, sedatives, or excessive oxygen; watch for **hypokalaemia** (β2-agonist + steroid) and **lactic acidosis** from high-dose β2-agonists.

> [!tip] 🎯 High-Yield Recall
> - Bundle = **O₂ (SpO₂ 93–95%) + salbutamol 4–10 puffs/2.5 mg neb q20min ×3 + prednisolone 40–50 mg PO early**.
> - **Severe** → add **ipratropium 500 mcg neb q20min ×3**; not responding at 1 h / PEF < 60% → **MgSO₄ 2 g IV over 20 min** (nebulised Mg no longer recommended).
> - **Response ≥ 60% predicted PEF at 1 h** → discharge; **silent chest/cyanosis/drowsy** → ICU + intubate (ketamine, permissive hypercapnia).
> - **Discharge: never SABA-only** — start/continue ICS(-formoterol), finish 5–7 d prednisolone, action plan, follow-up 2–7 d.
> - CXR and antibiotics are **not routine**.
