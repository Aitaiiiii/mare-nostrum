# Pneumothorax (Spontaneous & Tension)

Air in the pleural space collapsing the underlying lung. The overriding ER task is to separate **tension pneumothorax** (a clinical, immediately life-threatening diagnosis needing decompression *before* imaging) from a stable spontaneous or traumatic pneumothorax, where management has shifted (BTS 2023) from a **size-based** to a **symptom- and risk-based** approach.

## 🧬 Classification & Pathophysiology

Pleural pressure is normally sub-atmospheric; a breach of the visceral or parietal pleura lets air in until the pressure gradient equalises and the lung recoils inward. In **tension** physiology a one-way valve admits air on inspiration but not expiration, so intrapleural pressure rises above atmospheric, shifting the mediastinum, kinking the great veins, and collapsing venous return → obstructive shock and cardiac arrest.

| Type | Setting |
|---|---|
| **Primary spontaneous (PSP)** | no clinically apparent lung disease; tall thin young smokers; rupture of apical subpleural blebs |
| **Secondary spontaneous (SSP)** | underlying lung disease — **COPD/emphysema** (most common), asthma, TB, PCP, ILD, cystic fibrosis, necrotising pneumonia, lung cancer; more dangerous because reserve is low |
| **Traumatic** | penetrating/blunt chest trauma, rib fracture; often haemopneumothorax |
| **Iatrogenic** | central line, thoracentesis, transbronchial/lung biopsy, barotrauma from mechanical ventilation |
| **Catamenial** | thoracic endometriosis, perimenstrual, right-sided |

> [!note] 💎 Pearls
> - **Post-partum / perimenstrual right pneumothorax** in a woman → think **catamenial** (thoracic endometriosis).
> - A ventilated patient who suddenly desaturates with rising airway pressures and hypotension has a **tension pneumothorax until proven otherwise** — don't wait for a film.

## 🩺 Clinical presentation

Sudden pleuritic chest pain and dyspnoea; signs are **reduced/absent breath sounds, hyperresonant percussion, and reduced chest expansion** on the affected side. **Tension** adds **haemodynamic compromise** — hypotension, marked tachycardia, distended neck veins, tracheal deviation *away* from the side (a late sign), severe hypoxia, and cardiac arrest. SSP patients decompensate out of proportion to the size because of poor baseline reserve.

## 🩻 Investigations

- **Tension pneumothorax is a CLINICAL diagnosis — do not delay decompression for a chest X-ray.**
- **Erect CXR** confirms a stable pneumothorax: visceral pleural line with absent lung markings peripherally. Measure the interpleural distance (BTS measures at the hilum) for description, but note **size no longer dictates treatment by itself**.
- **CT chest** is the most sensitive — for small/loculated pneumothoraces, complex trauma, distinguishing bullae from pneumothorax before drainage, and surgical planning.
- **Point-of-care ultrasound** (absent lung sliding, absent B-lines, "lung point") is sensitive in the resuscitation room, especially supine trauma where the CXR is insensitive.
- Blood gas if hypoxic; pre-procedure clotting if intervention planned.

## Management (BTS 2023 — symptom- and risk-based)

**1. Tension pneumothorax → immediate needle decompression, then a chest drain.** Insert a large-bore cannula into the **2nd intercostal space, mid-clavicular line**, *or* the **4th/5th intercostal space, anterior-to-mid axillary line** (ATLS-preferred in adults, as the anterior chest wall is often too thick at the 2nd space for the cannula to reach the pleura). A rush of air confirms it. Follow immediately with a definitive **intercostal chest drain**. Give high-flow oxygen.

**2. Stable spontaneous pneumothorax — decide on two things: symptoms and high-risk characteristics, not size.**
- **High-risk characteristics (any one) → chest drain:** haemodynamic compromise, significant hypoxia, bilateral pneumothorax, underlying lung disease, **age ≥ 50 with a significant smoking history**, and haemopneumothorax.
- **No high-risk characteristics:**
  - **Minimally symptomatic / asymptomatic (no significant pain or breathlessness, no physiological compromise) → conservative observation, regardless of size** — this now applies to selected PSP *and* SSP. Reassess clinically and radiologically; many resolve.
  - **Symptomatic → intervention:** **needle aspiration** (14–16 G, aspirate up to 2.5 L, stop at resistance/cough) *or* **ambulatory management with a one-way-valve device** (e.g. Heimlich/pleural vent) where expertise and follow-up exist, *or* a **small-bore chest drain** (Seldinger, ≤ 14 F). Needle aspiration and ambulatory devices shorten hospital stay versus a standard drain with no more recurrence.

**3. Chest drain principles.** Insert within the **"safe triangle"** (anterior border of latissimus dorsi, lateral border of pectoralis major, 5th intercostal space / nipple line, apex below the axilla), above the rib to avoid the neurovascular bundle. Connect to an underwater seal; **do not clamp a bubbling drain**. Small-bore Seldinger drains are first-line for most spontaneous and iatrogenic pneumothoraces. Persistent air leak > 3–5 days → thoracic surgery review. **Suction is not routine**; add only for non-resolution, under senior guidance, using high-volume low-pressure systems.

**4. Recurrence prevention (definitive).** Refer for **VATS with bullectomy + pleurodesis (mechanical/talc)** after: a second ipsilateral (or first contralateral/bilateral) pneumothorax, persistent air leak/failure to re-expand, first episode in high-risk occupations (pilots, divers), or SSP where a recurrence would be dangerous. Chemical pleurodesis is an option for SSP recurrence prevention when surgery is unsuitable.

**5. Advice.** No air travel until radiographic resolution (guidance commonly 1–2 weeks after confirmed resolution); **never scuba dive again** after a spontaneous pneumothorax unless definitive bilateral surgical pleurectomy has been performed; smoking cessation reduces recurrence.

## 🇹🇭 Thailand availability

No specific drug therapy is involved; all equipment (needles, small- and large-bore Seldinger and surgical chest drains, underwater-seal and ambulatory one-way-valve devices) is standard in Thai hospitals, with VATS available at tertiary centres. TB and prior TB are common causes of **SSP** in Thailand — a spontaneous pneumothorax in an at-risk patient warrants evaluation for active/old TB and, in the immunocompromised, PCP.

> [!danger] 🚨 Red flags / strict avoidance
> - **Tension pneumothorax** (hypotension + absent breath sounds + hyperresonance ± tracheal deviation) → **decompress immediately, do NOT wait for a CXR**.
> - **Never clamp a bubbling chest drain** — it can convert a simple to a tension pneumothorax.
> - **SSP decompensates disproportionately** — treat and admit more aggressively than PSP; low threshold for a drain.
> - **No diving ever** after spontaneous pneumothorax (unless definitive surgery); no flying until resolution.

> [!tip] 🎯 High-Yield Recall
> - **Tension = clinical Dx → needle decompress** (2nd ICS MCL **or** 4th/5th ICS ant-mid axillary), then chest drain.
> - **BTS 2023 = symptoms + high-risk characteristics, not size.** High-risk (→ drain): haemodynamic compromise, significant hypoxia, bilateral, underlying lung disease, **≥ 50 + smoker**, haemopneumothorax.
> - **Minimally symptomatic → conservative regardless of size** (PSP and selected SSP).
> - **Symptomatic, low-risk → needle aspiration / ambulatory device / small-bore (≤ 14 F) drain.**
> - Drain in the **safe triangle**; recurrent/persistent leak → **VATS + pleurodesis**. No diving ever; no flying till resolved.
