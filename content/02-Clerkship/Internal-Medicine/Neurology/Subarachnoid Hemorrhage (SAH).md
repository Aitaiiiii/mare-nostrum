# Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (SAH)

Bleeding into the subarachnoid space, most often from a **ruptured saccular (berry) aneurysm** — a neurological catastrophe where the missed diagnosis is a small "warning" bleed that heralds a fatal re-rupture. The rule: **a sudden severe ("thunderclap") headache is SAH until excluded**, and once confirmed the aneurysm must be secured urgently.

## 🧬 Aetiology & Pathophysiology

~80% of spontaneous SAH is aneurysmal (rupture at branch points of the circle of Willis — ant. communicating, post. communicating, MCA); others: perimesencephalic (benign, venous), AVM, arterial dissection, and trauma (the commonest overall cause of subarachnoid blood, but managed as trauma). Rupture → abrupt ICP spike, global cerebral hypoperfusion, and blood in the CSF. The later dangers are **re-bleeding** (highest in the first 24 h), **hydrocephalus** (blood blocking CSF flow), and **delayed cerebral ischaemia (DCI)/vasospasm** at days 4–14.

Risk factors: hypertension, smoking, heavy alcohol, family history, polycystic kidney disease, connective-tissue disease.

## 🩺 Clinical presentation

**Sudden, severe "worst headache of my life" (thunderclap), maximal within seconds to minutes**, often with vomiting, neck stiffness/meningism, photophobia, transient or persistent loss of consciousness, and seizures. A **sentinel ("warning") headache** may precede rupture by days–weeks. Focal signs and a **third-nerve palsy (pupil-involving) suggest a posterior communicating artery aneurysm**. Grade severity with **Hunt-Hess / WFNS** (clinical) and **modified Fisher** (CT blood burden → vasospasm risk).

## 🩻 Investigations — the diagnostic pathway

- **Non-contrast CT head first.** Sensitivity is **~100% within 6 h** of onset, declining thereafter (blood degrades).
- **If CT is negative but suspicion persists → lumbar puncture** (ideally ≥ 6–12 h after onset): look for **xanthochromia** (CSF bilirubin from breakdown of RBCs — distinguishes true SAH from a traumatic tap) and a raised RBC count that does not clear tube-to-tube. (CT-angiography is an alternative pathway in some protocols.)
- **CT/catheter angiography** to identify and characterise the aneurysm for treatment planning once SAH is confirmed.
- Bloods, coagulation, ECG (SAH causes neurogenic ECG changes/troponin rise/stunned myocardium).

## 💊 Management

**1. Secure the aneurysm early.** **Prompt identification and treatment of the ruptured aneurysm, preferably within 24 hours** — by **endovascular coiling or surgical clipping** (coiling favoured when the anatomy suits). Manage in a **high-volume neurocritical-care centre** (lower mortality). Until secured, minimise re-bleed risk.

**2. Blood pressure — control until the aneurysm is secured.** Lower and control BP to reduce re-bleeding while avoiding hypotension (commonly keep **SBP < ~160 mmHg** pre-securing) with a titratable IV agent; avoid wide swings. After securing, permissive/higher pressures are allowed (and are used to treat DCI).

**3. Nimodipine — for delayed cerebral ischaemia, not the bleed.** **Nimodipine 60 mg PO/NG every 4 hours for 21 days** improves neurological outcome after aSAH (reduces DCI). It is neuroprotective — it does **not** prevent angiographic vasospasm per se; hold/reduce if it causes hypotension.

**4. Prevent/treat the complications.**
- **Hydrocephalus** (decreased consciousness) → **external ventricular drain (EVD)**.
- **Delayed cerebral ischaemia / vasospasm (days 4–14)** → maintain **euvolaemia and induced hypertension** (the old "triple-H" is replaced by euvolaemia + induced hypertension in the secured patient); endovascular therapy (intra-arterial vasodilator/angioplasty) for refractory DCI. Monitor with exam ± transcranial Doppler/CTA.
- **Seizures** → treat clinical seizures; **antiseizure medication for 7 days for new-onset seizures** after aSAH (not indefinite prophylaxis).
- Manage hyponatraemia (cerebral salt wasting/SIADH — do not fluid-restrict a hypovolaemic SAH patient), neurogenic cardiac/pulmonary effects, fever, and glucose.

## 🇹🇭 Thailand availability

Nimodipine, IV antihypertensives, and both endovascular coiling and surgical clipping are available at Thai neurovascular/tertiary centres; EVD and neuro-ICU care likewise. Peripheral hospitals should diagnose (CT ± LP), control BP, start nimodipine, and **transfer urgently to a centre that can secure the aneurysm.**

> [!danger] 🚨 Red flags / strict avoidance
> - **Thunderclap headache = SAH until excluded** — CT, and **LP for xanthochromia if CT is negative** and suspicion remains. Do not discharge a thunderclap headache on a normal CT alone if late-presenting/high-suspicion.
> - **Re-bleeding is highest in the first 24 h** → control BP and **secure the aneurysm within 24 h**.
> - **Nimodipine is for DCI outcome, not the acute bleed** — 60 mg q4h ×21 d; watch for hypotension.
> - **DCI/vasospasm at days 4–14** → euvolaemia + induced hypertension (once secured); do NOT fluid-restrict.
> - A **pupil-involving third-nerve palsy** = posterior communicating aneurysm → urgent imaging.
