# Spinal Cord Compression & Cauda Equina Syndrome

Compression of the spinal cord (above L1–L2) or the cauda equina (lumbosacral roots below the conus) — a **"function-is-time" emergency**: the neurological deficit at the moment of decompression is the strongest predictor of final function, so recognition and treatment must precede fixed paralysis and irreversible sphincter loss. Most cases in adults are **metastatic (malignant spinal cord compression, MSCC)**.

## 🧬 Causes & Pathophysiology

Compression → venous congestion, vasogenic oedema, ischaemia, and finally infarction of cord/roots. Causes:
- **Malignant (MSCC) — commonest:** vertebral metastasis with epidural extension/vertebral collapse — **breast, lung, prostate, myeloma, renal, lymphoma** (a first presentation of cancer in ~20%).
- **Infective:** **epidural abscess** (fever, IVDU, diabetes, recent procedure — a surgical emergency), TB (Pott's disease — important in Thailand).
- **Degenerative/traumatic:** large central disc prolapse (classic **cauda equina**), fracture, canal stenosis; haematoma (anticoagulation).

## 🩺 Clinical presentation — the red flags

- **Back pain** — often the first symptom, worse on lying/at night, may be band-like/radicular; new or escalating pain in a cancer patient is MSCC until excluded.
- **Motor:** limb weakness, gait disturbance; a **sensory level** localises cord compression.
- **Cauda equina red flags:** **bilateral sciatica, saddle (perineal) anaesthesia, bladder dysfunction (retention then overflow incontinence), faecal incontinence/loss of anal tone, and lower-limb weakness.**
- **Bladder/bowel dysfunction and saddle anaesthesia are late signs** — waiting for them costs recovery.

> [!note] 💎 Pearls
> - **Urinary retention with overflow** is the most consistent objective sign of established cauda equina — check a post-void residual.
> - A **thoracic sensory level** + bilateral leg weakness = cord compression; do not localise by the pain level alone (compression is often above it).

## 🩻 Investigations

- **Urgent whole-spine MRI within 24 hours** is the investigation of choice (multiple levels are common; imaging only the painful level misses skip lesions). CT/CT-myelogram if MRI contraindicated.
- **Post-void residual bladder volume/scan** (objectivises retention).
- Cancer work-up if new: bloods, **PSA, myeloma screen (SPEP/light chains — see [[Multiple Myeloma (MM)]])**, CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, biopsy; inflammatory markers/blood cultures if abscess suspected (MRI with contrast).

## 💊 Management

**1. Corticosteroid immediately (for MSCC).** **Dexamethasone 16 mg/day (e.g. 16 mg once or 8 mg twice daily) IV/PO** started on clinical suspicion, with a **PPI** and glucose monitoring — reduces oedema and can preserve/improve function while definitive treatment is arranged. (Avoid high-dose steroid before biopsy if lymphoma is likely and tissue is needed — discuss with oncology; but do not delay in progressing deficit.)

**2. Definitive treatment — urgent, multidisciplinary.**
- **Surgical decompression (± stabilisation) followed by radiotherapy** improves ambulation versus radiotherapy alone in suitable candidates with a single compressive level, good performance status, and reasonable prognosis (Patchell trial).
- **Radiotherapy alone** for multiple levels, radiosensitive tumours, poor surgical candidates, or established complete paraplegia.
- **Epidural abscess / unstable fracture / haematoma → emergency surgery.** **TB (Pott's) → anti-TB ± surgery.**

**3. Supportive & the neuro-oncology bundle.** Analgesia, **VTE prophylaxis**, bladder care (catheter for retention), pressure-area and bowel care, spinal precautions/immobilisation if unstable, and early oncology/neurosurgery/radiation-oncology involvement. Identify the primary and treat systemically.

**4. Cauda equina from disc prolapse → emergency neurosurgical decompression** (ideally within 24–48 h of onset, sooner if incomplete) — the window to preserve bladder/bowel function is short.

## 🇹🇭 Thailand availability

Dexamethasone, MRI, radiotherapy, and spinal surgery are available at Thai tertiary centres; peripheral hospitals should **start dexamethasone, image (or refer for MRI), and transfer urgently.** **Spinal TB (Pott's disease) is an important Thai cause** of cord compression (subacute, with a gibbus/paraspinal collections) and needs anti-TB therapy ± surgical decompression, so TB and metastasis are both high on the Thai differential.

> [!danger] 🚨 Red flags / strict avoidance
> - **New/escalating back pain in a cancer patient = MSCC until excluded** → urgent whole-spine MRI + dexamethasone.
> - **Cauda equina red flags** (saddle anaesthesia, bladder/bowel dysfunction, bilateral sciatica, ↓anal tone) → emergency imaging + decompression; **check a post-void residual**.
> - **Bladder/bowel signs are late** — do not wait for them; the pre-treatment deficit predicts recovery.
> - **Image the WHOLE spine** — skip metastases are common; the pain level is not the compression level.
> - **Epidural abscess / unstable fracture → emergency surgery**; consider **spinal TB** in Thailand.

> [!tip] 🎯 High-Yield Recall
> - Back pain + **sensory level / weakness / bladder-bowel / saddle anaesthesia** → emergency.
> - **Whole-spine MRI within 24 h** (skip lesions); **post-void residual** for retention.
> - **Dexamethasone 16 mg/day + PPI** on suspicion (MSCC); then **surgery + RT** (Patchell) vs **RT alone**.
> - **Cauda equina from disc → emergency decompression** (window is short); epidural abscess → surgery; TB → anti-TB ± surgery.
> - Deficit at treatment predicts outcome — **speed is everything.**
