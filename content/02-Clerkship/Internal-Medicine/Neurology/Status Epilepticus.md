# Status Epilepticus

A single seizure lasting **≥ 5 minutes**, or recurrent seizures **without recovery of consciousness between them**, is convulsive status epilepticus (SE) — a time-defined emergency. The operational threshold is 5 minutes (not 30) because seizures rarely self-terminate beyond that and neuronal injury and pharmacoresistance accrue with time. The whole game is **treating fast and at adequate dose** — under-dosing benzodiazepines is the commonest error.

## 🧬 Pathophysiology & causes

Sustained seizure activity → failure of GABAergic inhibition, internalisation of GABA-A receptors (explaining benzodiazepine resistance as time passes), excitotoxic neuronal injury, and systemic effects (hyperthermia, lactic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, hypoxia, aspiration). Always search for a cause:

- **Low levels/withdrawal** — antiseizure-medication non-adherence (commonest in known epilepsy), [[Alcohol Withdrawal|alcohol]]/benzodiazepine withdrawal.
- **Acute brain insult** — stroke/[[Intracerebral Hemorrhage (ICH)|haemorrhage]], [[Bacterial Meningitis|CNS infection]]/encephalitis, trauma, tumour, [[Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (SAH)|SAH]], hypoxic-ischaemic injury.
- **Metabolic** — **hypoglycaemia**, hyponatraemia, hypocalcaemia, uraemia, hepatic failure, hyperthermia.
- **Toxic** — drugs/overdose (e.g. isoniazid → give pyridoxine), eclampsia.

## 🩺 Presentation

Generalised tonic-clonic activity is obvious; also recognise **subtle/non-convulsive SE** — a persistently obtunded or "not-waking" patient after a convulsion (or a critically ill patient with unexplained coma) may be in **non-convulsive status**, diagnosed on **EEG**. Focal motor SE (epilepsia partialis continua) and absence SE are other forms.

## 🩻 Investigations (in parallel with treatment — never delay drugs)

**Immediate bedside:** capillary **glucose** (treat hypoglycaemia at once), ABC, SpO₂, temperature. **Bloods:** electrolytes (Na, Ca, Mg), renal/liver function, CK, glucose, **antiseizure-drug levels**, toxicology, blood gas, and a **pregnancy test** (eclampsia). **Neuroimaging (CT)** once stabilised for a structural cause; **LP** if CNS infection suspected (after imaging/coagulation as needed); **EEG** if not waking (non-convulsive SE) or for refractory SE monitoring.

## 💊 Management — a timed, staged protocol

**0–5 min — stabilise.** ABC, high-flow O₂, IV access, monitor; **check glucose** and give **glucose (with thiamine first in the malnourished/alcoholic)** if low; treat obvious cause (Na, eclampsia → magnesium, isoniazid → pyridoxine).

**5–20 min — first-line: an adequately-dosed benzodiazepine (do NOT under-dose).**
- **IV lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg (up to 4 mg), may repeat once**, OR
- **IM midazolam 10 mg** (≥ 40 kg) — the drug of choice when there is no IV access, OR
- **IV diazepam 0.15–0.2 mg/kg (up to 10 mg), may repeat once.**
(ESETT/RAMPART lesson: patients are routinely under-dosed — give the full mg/kg dose.)

**20–40 min — second-line: an IV antiseizure medication (ESETT showed the three are equivalent — choose by patient/availability):**
- **Levetiracetam 60 mg/kg IV (max 4.5 g)**, or
- **Valproate 40 mg/kg IV (max 3 g)** — avoid in hepatic failure/pregnancy, or
- **(Fos)phenytoin 20 mg PE/kg IV** — cardiac monitoring, watch hypotension/arrhythmia and extravasation.

**> 40 min — refractory SE → anaesthetic infusion + intubation, continuous EEG:**
- **Midazolam, propofol, or pentobarbital/thiopental infusion**, titrated to seizure suppression/burst-suppression on EEG; ICU, ventilate, support BP.
- **Super-refractory SE** (persisting ≥ 24 h or recurring on weaning anaesthesia) → ketamine, immunotherapy if autoimmune, and treat the underlying cause aggressively.

**Throughout:** treat hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, and aspiration; correct the precipitant; continue a maintenance antiseizure medication.

## 🇹🇭 Thailand availability

Lorazepam, diazepam, midazolam, levetiracetam, valproate, phenytoin, phenobarbital, propofol, and thiopental are all available in Thai EDs/ICUs (IV lorazepam supply can be variable — diazepam/midazolam are reliable alternatives). The **Thai epilepsy CPG (2564/2021)** is on disk in the reference library; management mirrors the AES timed protocol. Consider **CNS infection (incl. TB, Japanese encephalitis), eclampsia, and toxic causes** prominently in the Thai differential.

> [!danger] 🚨 Red flags / strict avoidance
> - **Treat at 5 minutes, at full benzodiazepine dose** — under-dosing is the leading error and drives refractoriness.
> - **Always check and treat glucose** (thiamine before glucose in the alcoholic/malnourished).
> - **Not waking after convulsions = possible non-convulsive SE → EEG**; don't assume "post-ictal".
> - **Refractory SE (> 40 min) → intubate + anaesthetic infusion + continuous EEG** — don't keep repeating boluses indefinitely.
> - Find the cause: hypoglycaemia, Na⁺, eclampsia (Mg), isoniazid (pyridoxine), CNS infection, withdrawal.

> [!tip] 🎯 High-Yield Recall
> - **SE = seizure ≥ 5 min or no recovery between seizures** → treat now.
> - **Benzodiazepine first, full dose**: lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg IV / **midazolam 10 mg IM (no IV)** / diazepam 0.15–0.2 mg/kg.
> - **Second-line by ~20 min (ESETT-equivalent): levetiracetam 60 mg/kg · valproate 40 mg/kg · fosphenytoin 20 PE/kg**.
> - **Refractory > 40 min → anaesthetic infusion (midazolam/propofol/pentobarbital) + intubate + cEEG**.
> - Check glucose (thiamine first if alcoholic); think eclampsia (Mg), INH (pyridoxine), CNS infection; EEG if not waking.
