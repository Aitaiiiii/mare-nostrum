# Alcohol Withdrawal & Delirium Tremens

The syndrome that follows abrupt cessation/reduction of alcohol in a dependent person, spanning a benign tremor to **delirium tremens (DTs)** — a hyperadrenergic delirium with genuine mortality. Two rules govern the ER: **benzodiazepines are first-line and dosed to the patient's symptoms**, and **thiamine is given before any glucose** to avoid precipitating Wernicke encephalopathy.

## 🧬 Pathophysiology & timeline

Chronic alcohol enhances inhibitory **GABA-A** tone and suppresses excitatory **NMDA-glutamate** signalling; the brain compensates by down-regulating GABA and up-regulating NMDA receptors. Sudden withdrawal unmasks an **unopposed excitatory, hyperadrenergic state** → the escalating syndrome:

| Time after last drink | Feature |
|---|---|
| **6–12 h** | minor withdrawal — tremor, anxiety, sweating, tachycardia, nausea, insomnia |
| **12–24 h** | **alcoholic hallucinosis** — visual/tactile/auditory hallucinations with **intact sensorium** (distinguishes from DTs) |
| **24–48 h** | **withdrawal seizures** — generalised tonic-clonic ("rum fits"), usually brief/self-limited |
| **48–72+ h** | **delirium tremens** — global confusion/delirium, agitation, hallucinations, fever, marked autonomic instability (tachycardia, hypertension, diaphoresis); mortality if untreated |

Risk factors for severe withdrawal/DTs: prior DTs or withdrawal seizures, high/prolonged intake, older age, comorbidity, and a high presenting CIWA-Ar.

## 🩺 Assessment

Use the **CIWA-Ar** score (10 items — nausea, tremor, sweats, anxiety, agitation, tactile/auditory/visual disturbance, headache, orientation) to grade severity and drive treatment. Screen for the complications that masquerade as or worsen withdrawal: **head injury/subdural** (fell while intoxicated), **infection/sepsis**, **hepatic encephalopathy** ([[Cirrhosis]]), electrolyte derangement (**hypomagnesaemia, hypokalaemia, hypophosphataemia**), hypoglycaemia, GI bleed, and pancreatitis.

## 🩻 Investigations

Glucose, electrolytes including **magnesium and phosphate**, renal and liver function, CBC, coagulation; consider CT head (new focal signs, seizure, trauma, or failure to improve), septic screen, and ECG. **Alcohol level does not exclude withdrawal** — a dependent patient can withdraw at a still-positive level.

## 💊 Management

**1. Thiamine BEFORE glucose.** Give **thiamine (IV/IM) before or with any glucose-containing fluid** — a glucose load in a thiamine-deficient brain can precipitate **Wernicke encephalopathy**. For prophylaxis, **thiamine ≥ 100 mg**; if **Wernicke is suspected** (ophthalmoplegia/nystagmus, ataxia, confusion), give **high-dose thiamine 500 mg IV q8h for 72 h**, then step down. Replace **magnesium** (potentiates thiamine and lowers seizure threshold when low).

**2. Benzodiazepines — first-line for all stages.**
- **Symptom-triggered dosing guided by CIWA-Ar** (dose when the score crosses a threshold) reduces total benzodiazepine and duration versus fixed schedules in stable ward patients.
- **Long-acting agents (diazepam, chlordiazepoxide)** give a smoother, self-tapering course; **lorazepam** (or oxazepam) is preferred in **significant liver disease/elderly** (no active metabolites, non-hepatic metabolism).
- **Severe withdrawal / DTs → front-loaded, escalating IV benzodiazepine** to light sedation; give parenterally; there is no ceiling — titrate to control.

**3. Refractory DTs.** **Phenobarbital** (experienced protocols) or a benzodiazepine infusion; **escalate to ICU**, and for benzodiazepine-resistant cases consider **propofol/dexmedetomidine as adjuncts** (dexmedetomidine controls autonomic features but does not prevent seizures — never use it as the sole agent). Manage airway.

**4. Supportive.** Correct **K⁺, Mg²⁺, PO₄³⁻**, fluids, treat the precipitant/comorbidity, and provide a calm low-stimulus environment. Withdrawal seizures are treated with benzodiazepines — **long-term anticonvulsants are not indicated** for uncomplicated withdrawal seizures. Arrange addiction follow-up.

## 🇹🇭 Thailand availability

Diazepam, lorazepam, chlordiazepoxide, thiamine, magnesium sulfate, and phenobarbital are all on the Thai NLEM and available in Thai EDs/wards. Alcohol-use disorder is common in Thailand; the same principles apply, with attention to the frequent overlap with **cirrhosis/hepatic encephalopathy** (favour lorazepam and watch for precipitants) and to trauma from intoxication.

> [!danger] 🚨 Red flags / strict avoidance
> - **Give thiamine BEFORE glucose** — glucose first can precipitate Wernicke encephalopathy.
> - **DTs is a medical emergency** with real mortality → aggressive front-loaded benzodiazepines + ICU.
> - **Benzodiazepines are first-line** — do not treat withdrawal agitation with antipsychotics alone (lower seizure threshold, don't treat the GABA deficit).
> - **Don't anchor on "just drunk/withdrawing"** — exclude head injury/subdural, sepsis, hepatic encephalopathy, hypoglycaemia, and electrolyte derangement.
> - **Dexmedetomidine is an adjunct only** — it does not prevent withdrawal seizures.

> [!tip] 🎯 High-Yield Recall
> - Timeline: tremor 6–12 h → hallucinosis 12–24 h (**clear sensorium**) → seizures 24–48 h → **DTs 48–72+ h** (delirium + autonomic storm).
> - **Benzodiazepines first-line, symptom-triggered by CIWA-Ar**; long-acting (diazepam/chlordiazepoxide), **lorazepam if liver disease**; front-load for DTs.
> - **Thiamine before glucose**; Wernicke → **500 mg IV q8h ×72 h**; replace **Mg/K/PO₄**.
> - Refractory DTs → **phenobarbital** ± infusion, ICU.
> - Uncomplicated withdrawal seizures need benzodiazepines, **not** chronic anticonvulsants.
